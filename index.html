Track width
Side-to-side distance between wheel centerlines, in inches. Not used by the anti-squat or spring rate math, but it's a vehicle-level fact front-end tools want — Steering Geometry reads it for context, and it's here once so a future tool (an Ackermann checker, say) doesn't need its own copy either.
CG height above ground
The hardest number here to get right without real equipment — there's no simple scale trick for it the way there is for corner weight. A tilt-table measurement is the accurate way; short of that, treat this as an estimate and revisit it once you can measure it.
Corner weights
Static weight at each of the four corners, in lb, ideally from scales. Feeds Spring & Bag Rate's wheel-rate and ride-frequency math directly, and also drives the CG fore-aft calculation below.
CG, forward of rear axle (computed)
Unlike CG height, this one doesn't need a separate manual measurement — it falls out of the corner weights you already entered: heavier front corners relative to the total shift the CG forward, and the wheelbase sets the scale. That's exactly what (front total ÷ total weight) × wheelbase computes. This feeds 4-Link Geometry's anti-squat calculation.

Getting Started

Ride Bench has one shared Vehicle Profile and two calculators that read from it. This page walks through the order to use them in.

Step 1: Fill in your Vehicle Profile

Wheelbase, tire radius, CG height, and all four corner weights live in one place now — the Vehicle Profile view — and both calculators pull from it live. That makes it the first stop, every time.

CG fore-aft position isn't something you enter — it's calculated automatically from the corner weights and wheelbase, so it can't fall out of sync with them.

CG height has no simple measuring trick: tilt-table it if you can, otherwise it's an estimate. Corner weights are different — they need the truck built, rolling, and in near-final form (frame cut, bed mounted, drivetrain in) to mean anything. If the truck isn't there yet, placeholder numbers are fine for exploring the tools, but hold off on real spring or bag decisions until you have real weights.

Step 2: 4-Link Geometry

  1. Place (drag) the four pivots — Fu/Au for the upper link, Fl/Al for the lower link — to match real or planned bracket locations. This can be done before the truck is rolling, since it's driving where you'll put brackets, not something you measure after the fact.
  2. Read Anti-Squat % at travel = 0 as your baseline number.
  3. Run the travel slider through bump and droop and watch anti-squat, the instant center, and pinion angle change shift — geometry that looks good at rest can look very different at the extremes of travel.

Step 3: Spring & Bag Rate

  1. Pick Coil spring or Air spring (bag) mode, and select which corner you're calculating — its weight comes from the Vehicle Profile automatically now.
  2. Fill in the spring/bag fields: rate and motion ratio for coil; pressure, area, volume, and motion ratio for air.
  3. Read Wheel Rate first — it accounts for motion ratio, unlike the raw spring rate.
  4. Read Ride Frequency last — it's the number that best predicts actual ride feel. Rear is usually tuned a bit above front.
This tool's numbers are only as good as the corner weights sitting in the Vehicle Profile, so real scale weights matter more here than they do in the geometry tool.
One thing to remember
Since both calculators now read the same Vehicle Profile, correcting a number there updates both automatically — there's no more risk of the two tools disagreeing. The catch is that this only holds if the Vehicle Profile itself is kept current as the build progresses: re-weigh and update it once the truck is finished, not just once early on.

Pivot coordinates

Origin is the rear axle centerline at ride height, ground level. Drag pivots on the diagram or type exact values here.
What do these settings mean?
Fu / Au — the upper link
Fu is where the upper link bolts to the frame. Au is where that same link bolts to the axle. The line between them is the upper link. On most trucks this link is shorter and steeper than the lower one — that steep angle is a big part of what generates anti-squat.
Fl / Al — the lower link
Fl is the lower link's frame-side mount, Al its axle-side mount. This link is usually longer and closer to flat, and it does most of the work of locating the axle fore-aft under acceleration and braking.
Instant center (IC)
Extend the upper link's line and the lower link's line until they cross — that crossing point is the instant center. It's a purely geometric construction (not a physical part) that tells you, for an instant, how the axle "wants" to move. Move the pivots and the IC moves with them.
Anti-squat %
Draw a line from the rear tire's contact patch through the instant center, and see how high that line is by the time it reaches the truck's center of gravity (CG). Compare that height to the actual CG height — that ratio, as a percent, is anti-squat. 100% means the rear geometry fully cancels the weight transfer that squats the rear under acceleration. Under 100% still squats some; well over 100% starts to jack the rear up instead of just resisting squat. For a street cruiser, most builders aim roughly 60–110%.
Travel slider (droop / bump)
This doesn't just redraw a picture — it re-solves the actual linkage. Compression (bump) and extension (droop) are entered as degrees of lower-link rotation. The tool then works out where the upper pivot has to land so both links stay their real, fixed length — the same constraint the physical links are under. Watch how anti-squat and the instant center shift as the suspension moves; a design that looks good sitting still can change a lot at ride height extremes.
Pinion angle change
As the linkage moves through travel, the axle housing rotates. This shows how many degrees the axle (and therefore the pinion/driveshaft angle) has rotated relative to its position at static ride height — useful for checking you won't be fighting driveshaft vibration at either end of travel.
Wheelbase / CG position / CG height / tire radius
These describe the truck itself, not the linkage, and now live in the shared Vehicle Profile view rather than here — this page just reads them (see the "Using:" line above). CG fore-aft is calculated automatically from corner weights; CG height is the one number here that's still a manual estimate until you can measure it some other way.
Limits of this model: this is a 2D side-view approximation. It ignores lateral link angles (roll steer, side-loading), bushing compliance, and how anti-squat interacts with anti-lift under braking. Treat it as a way to compare link layouts and sanity-check geometry before you cut brackets — not a replacement for measuring the real truck.

What do these settings mean?
Spring rate vs. wheel rate
Spring rate is a property of the spring or bag alone — lb of force per inch it compresses, measured along its own axis. Wheel rate is what the wheel actually feels, measured along the wheel's travel. They're rarely the same number because the spring usually isn't mounted directly over the wheel.
Motion ratio
The ratio of wheel travel to spring travel at the spring's mounting point (wheel travel ÷ spring travel). Because the spring resists motion at its own end, not the wheel's, wheel rate scales with the square of motion ratio: wheel rate = spring rate × MR². A spring mounted at MR 0.7 (spring moves less than the wheel) gives a much softer wheel rate than the same spring at MR 1.0.
Ride frequency — and why it matters more than spring rate alone
Ride frequency is how many times per second (or cycles per minute) the sprung corner would bounce on its spring if disturbed. It folds wheel rate and the weight it's supporting into one number, which is why it's the number that actually tracks "ride quality" — the same wheel rate feels completely different under a light corner than a heavy one. Roughly: soft street/cruiser rides land around 1.0–1.5 Hz, firmer sport setups run 1.5–2.0 Hz, and race/off-road setups push higher still. Rear frequency is usually tuned slightly above front to help control pitch.
Static deflection / sag
How far the spring compresses under the corner's static weight alone — corner weight ÷ spring rate (measured at the spring, not the wheel). Useful for checking the spring isn't already near coil-bind or its free length before travel even starts.
Why an air spring's rate isn't constant (unlike a coil)
A coil's rate is fixed by its wire and geometry. An air spring's rate comes from compressing a gas, so it climbs as the bag is loaded and pressure rises — there's no single "spring rate" for a bag, only a rate at a given pressure. This tool uses the standard adiabatic approximation k ≈ n·P·A² / V (n≈1.4, P absolute pressure, A effective area, V internal volume) to estimate that instantaneous rate. Lower internal volume (small bag, no extra air tank) makes the rate climb faster with pressure; adding volume softens and flattens the curve.
Bag presets
The preset list plugs in rough effective-area and volume figures for a few common bag families so you can get a ballpark fast — swap to "Custom" and enter your bag's actual spec-sheet numbers (or measured volume including lines/tank) for anything you'll cut brackets against.
Limits of this model: both modes assume a simple linear (coil) or adiabatic-gas (air) spring in isolation — they don't account for bump stops, droop limiters, helper springs, leaf-spring progressive rate, or a shock's own compression damping. Air spring area and volume also change somewhat through travel on a real bag; treat the output as a starting point for a given ride height, not a full travel curve.

Pivot coordinates

Origin is the axle centerline at ride height, ground level. Positive x is toward one side of the truck, negative the other — pick a consistent side when you measure. Drag pivots on the diagram or type exact values here.
What do these settings mean?
Tf / Ta — the track bar (panhard rod)
Tf is where the track bar mounts to the frame. Ta is where it mounts to the axle. The track bar's only job is locating the axle side-to-side; as the suspension moves through travel, the axle arcs around Tf, since Ta is stuck the track bar's fixed length away from it.
Df / Da — the drag link
Df is the drag link's frame-side end — effectively the pitman arm's output, hanging off the steering box. Da is where it connects to the steering arm on the axle knuckle. Turning the steering wheel moves Df in an arc around the steering box; that same link also has to tolerate the axle moving through suspension travel without steering the truck on its own.
Why the two links' geometry needs to match
As the axle moves up and down, Ta swings on an arc centered on Tf. For the steering to stay quiet through that same travel, Da needs to swing on essentially the same arc — same effective radius and a similar mounting angle to Tf's. If the drag link's length and angle (Df to Da) don't closely match the track bar's (Tf to Ta), Da gets pulled along a different arc than Ta, and the steering arm — and therefore the front wheels — gets tugged left or right purely from suspension movement, independent of anything the driver does with the wheel.
Bump steer error
The horizontal (toe) displacement of the drag link's axle-side point (Da) relative to where it would sit if it had swung through an identical arc to the track bar's axle-side point (Ta) — same rotation, same center. Near zero at every point in travel means the two links are tracking each other well. A large or growing value means the axle steers itself as it cycles.
What bump steer feels like on the road
Wandering that isn't a driver input — needing small, constant steering corrections to hold a line, especially over washboard or repeated bumps, or a sudden unplanned tug on the wheel when one front tire drops into a dip mid-corner. It's the suspension moving the steering, not the other way around.
Travel slider (droop / bump)
Re-solves both arcs the same way the 4-Link tool re-solves its four-bar linkage: the track bar rotates around Tf by the travel angle, which fixes a new axle-side point Ta; the drag link and the rigid axle (Ta to Da, a fixed distance) are then solved together to find where Da lands. Watch bump steer error through the full range — geometry that's quiet near ride height can get much worse at the travel extremes.
Bump steer risk, full travel range
The largest bump steer error seen anywhere across the whole travel slider sweep, not just at the current position — because a design can look fine at ride height and still go bad at one end of travel. Flagged good/warn/bad the same way the 4-Link tool flags anti-squat.
Fixing it
The usual fix is relocating one of the frame-side pivots (Tf or Df) so the two links' lengths and mounting angles come closer together — not something to weld up and hope. Drag the pivots here and watch the error number before you commit to a bracket location.
Wheelbase / tire radius / track width
These describe the truck itself, not the steering linkage, and live in the shared Vehicle Profile view rather than here — this page just reads them for reference (see the "Using:" line above).
Limits of this model: this is a 2D front-view approximation, same spirit as the 4-Link tool's side-view one. It treats the track bar and drag link as simple rigid links pivoting in one plane, ignores bushing compliance, steering box lash, and Ackermann/tie-rod geometry on the rest of the steering linkage, and assumes the "identical arc" comparison as the standard for zero bump steer. Use it to compare pivot locations and sanity-check geometry before cutting brackets — not a replacement for driving the truck and feeling for it.

What do these settings mean?
Why the reference point matters
Ride height only means something if it's measured the same way every time — same point on the body (fender lip, rocker, frame rail, whatever you pick), same point on the ground, same tire pressure. Switching reference points between entries makes the log compare apples to oranges; pick one method and stick with it for this truck.
Front − rear (rake)
The difference between average front height and average rear height. A positive number means the front sits higher than the rear (nose-high); negative means nose-low. Rake affects headlight aim, aerodynamics, and sometimes handling balance — worth tracking alongside the raw corner numbers, not just each corner in isolation.
Log a baseline now, then log again after every change
This log is most useful as a before/after record. Log a baseline today even with rough or placeholder numbers — a rough baseline beats no baseline. Then log again any time something suspension-related changes (new springs, a bag pressure adjustment, added weight, worn bushings) so there's an actual measured record to check "did that change do what I expected" against, instead of relying on memory of "it used to sit higher."
Limits of this log: it just records what you type in — there's no cross-check against the other tools, and no unit conversion or averaging beyond the simple front/rear split above. Garbage in, garbage out: it's only as useful as the discipline of measuring the same way and logging consistently.

What this is showing

Nothing here is a new input — every pivot comes straight from what's already saved in 4-Link Geometry (rear) and Steering Geometry (front), and the frame width, wheelbase, and tire size come from Vehicle Profile. This view exists to show both ends of the truck moving through suspension travel at once, which neither 2D tool can do by itself since they're separate pages.
What do these settings mean?
Rear axle (from 4-Link Geometry)
The 4-Link tool's side-view linkage is drawn twice — mirrored to both sides of the vehicle centerline at half the track width — since a real 4-link rear normally has a symmetric upper/lower link pair on each side. A single axle tube connects the two sides at the linkage's average height and fore-aft position.
Front axle (from Steering Geometry)
Unlike the rear linkage, a track bar and drag link are not a mirrored left/right pair — there's exactly one of each, running at an angle across the truck. So instead of mirroring, this view places the track bar and drag link's actual pivots at their real left/right position directly, at the front axle's location (one wheelbase forward of the rear axle). An axle tube spans the full track width at their average height.
Travel slider
Drives both axles through the same bump/droop range as the 2D tools, using the exact same four-bar linkage solve (this view, 4-Link Geometry, and Steering Geometry all call the same shared solver, so the numbers agree). As in the 2D diagrams, the frame stays fixed and the axle moves relative to it — so at extreme travel a tire can appear to dip below or lift off the ground plane. That's a simplification carried over from the 2D tools, not a new bug here.
Show rear instant-center & extended link lines
Draws the same instant-center construction the 4-Link tool shows in its side view. Off by default because it's easier to read up close in 2D than from an orbiting 3D camera — Steering Geometry doesn't have a single "instant center" the same way (its two links each sweep their own arc), so this toggle only affects the rear.
Limits of this view: it's a visualization, not a new calculation — anti-squat and bump-steer numbers still live on their own 2D pages. Frame rail position/height, axle tube size, and tire width are drawn as reasonable simplified shapes, not real part dimensions. If 4-Link Geometry or Steering Geometry haven't been visited yet, this view falls back to their documented default pivots rather than erroring.
